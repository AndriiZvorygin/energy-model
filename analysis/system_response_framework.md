# System-Response Diagnostic Framework

## Purpose

The framework helps readers recognize observable symptoms of energy and oil constraints, trace how they can propagate through the economy, distinguish physical scarcity from demand destruction, and compare current conditions with historical episodes.

It is diagnostic and explanatory. It does not create a unified forecasting model, a social-instability score, or a replacement for the locked GM2-only lag-5 WTI/Brent benchmark.

## Five Layers

1. **Physical energy conditions:** production, consumption, inventories, comparative inventory and refinery constraints.
2. **Energy affordability and financial conditions:** real oil, household and GDP energy burden, energy inflation, income, rates, credit and GM2.
3. **Production, consumption, and investment response:** industry, manufacturing, spending, investment, GDP and productivity.
4. **Labour and household consequences:** hours, temporary work, employment structure, wages, sentiment and delinquency.
5. **Social and institutional symptoms:** documented in the catalogue as proposed research only; no social-stress model is implemented.

## Transmission Chain

`Liquidity -> energy demand -> physical tightness -> oil price and energy burden -> inflation and margins -> consumption and investment -> industrial production and GDP -> hours, wages and employment quality -> household and social stress`

Each arrow is a hypothesis to test separately. Correlation describes co-movement, out-of-sample improvement describes predictive usefulness, and neither alone establishes causality.

## First Release

The catalogue contains 37 measured, derived, proxy and proposed indicators; 30 have implemented or derived data paths. The monthly-aligned core extends to the latest available source observation (2026-07), but series have different release dates and the final month can be partial. Current-state rows preserve those indicator-specific dates, percentiles, confirming evidence, conflicts and confidence levels.

## The Affordability Squeeze: Why Scarcity Can Produce Lower Prices

This is a synthesis hypothesis, not a single validated model. Physical constraints or higher extraction, refining, transport, and financing costs can raise the delivered cost of energy and energy-dependent essentials. If household and business incomes do not rise enough to absorb those costs, discretionary spending, production, employment, travel, and investment can contract. Energy demand may then fall enough to lower benchmark crude prices even while delivered fuel costs, product shortages, or physical constraints remain difficult. Lower benchmark prices can weaken producer cash flow and reduce replacement investment, creating renewed vulnerability as depleted production continues to decline.

The framework keeps five concepts separate: **benchmark crude prices** are reference prices for a grade and location; **delivered consumer fuel and transport costs** include refining, shipping, financing, taxes, distribution, and retail margins; **physical availability** concerns whether the required energy products can be supplied; **affordability** compares those costs with household or business income; and **producer investment viability** asks whether realized prices and cash flow support operations and replacement supply. Shipping, refining, financing, taxation, and distribution costs can rise without producing an equivalent increase in the net price received by crude producers.

The proposed sequence is: physical constraint -> higher delivered essential costs -> reduced discretionary spending -> lower production and employment -> lower energy demand -> lower benchmark price -> reduced producer investment -> greater future supply vulnerability. Each link remains a separate empirical question. A low benchmark price can be a symptom of demand destruction rather than evidence of abundant, affordable energy.

This synthesis layer draws on three interpretive essays by Gail Tverberg. [Affordability, Not Scarcity, Is the Real Energy Crisis](https://ourfiniteworld.com/2026/08/05/affordability-not-scarcity-is-the-real-energy-crisis/) provides the broad affordability-centred interpretation, the producer-consumer price conflict, and the energy-growth-beyond-population framing. [Why Oil Shortages May Bring Lower Prices and Recession](https://ourfiniteworld.com/2026/06/29/why-oil-shortages-may-bring-lower-prices-and-recession/) describes the acute sequence in which a physical disruption can raise delivered costs, contract economic activity and demand, and later pull benchmark crude prices down. [The oil glut and low prices reflect an affordability problem](https://ourfiniteworld.com/2015/03/09/the-oil-glut-and-low-prices-reflect-an-affordability-problem/) is an earlier formulation of the affordability problem. These essays are hypothesis and synthesis framing, not statistical sources; the project's charts use EIA, BEA, BLS, FRED, Energy Institute, and OWID data.

## Regime Vocabulary

- **A. Expansion and rising demand:** activity and consumption rise without broad physical deficits.
- **B. Physical tightening:** inventories fall, utilization rises, or demand outruns supply.
- **C. Energy affordability stress:** real prices and expenditure burdens rise faster than income.
- **D. Economic transmission:** margins, spending, investment and industrial output weaken.
- **E. Demand destruction:** consumption and activity fall while affordability remains strained.
- **F. Price collapse:** oil falls because demand weakens or supply expands; the cause must be diagnosed.
- **G. Labour and household after-effects:** hours, flexible employment, real wages and credit quality deteriorate.
- **H. Recovery or renewed tightening:** burden eases and activity recovers, or demand re-tightens the physical market.

The observatory now publishes an **experimental transparent rule classifier** with separate provisional monthly and confirmed quarterly clocks. It shows contributing indicators, source dates, freshness, coverage, confidence, conflicts, threshold sensitivity and historical analogues, and it can return mixed or unclassified. The formal scope is U.S. energy-economic conditions with global oil-market and global-liquidity inputs. Latest-vintage historical tests are retrospective where real-time vintages are unavailable.

## Evidence Labels

- **Validated relationship:** passed the project's established validation or locked-model decision process.
- **Supported historical pattern:** recurs descriptively across episodes but is not a stable forecast claim.
- **Contextual historical pattern:** long-run context that helps frame system conditions without measuring welfare or establishing a forecast rule.
- **Contextual indicator:** useful for interpretation without demonstrated independent predictive value.
- **Experimental proxy:** measurable approximation requiring additional validation.
- **Scenario concept:** proposed layer without an implemented reproducible series.

## Data And Methodological Gaps

- Global spare capacity and a redistributable continuous futures curve are missing.
- Household burden is aggregate and does not reveal distributional exposure.
- Quarterly GDP, investment, credit and delinquency fields are latest-vintage and not release-time safe.
- Current-state percentiles use the available latest-vintage history and are not causal thresholds.
- Social and institutional measures are sparse, definition-sensitive and intentionally deferred.

## Recommended Next Step

Add real-time vintages and reliable global physical-balance measures, then implement transparent multi-indicator regime rules. Only after those rules survive expanding and rolling validation should the project test household-distribution or social-response models.
